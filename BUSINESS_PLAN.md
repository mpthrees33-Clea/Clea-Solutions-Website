# PART A — PRIVATE NEGOTIATION PREP

**🔒 Confidential. Do not share. This is your game plan, not a handout.**

## A1. The situation, in plain terms

- You are **being replaced as a sales rep — confirmed — around end of September /
  early October.** That is a hard deadline, not a maybe.
- You have built real, working AI for the company **for free**, as proof you can
  do it: PO IDP (purchase-order processing with 7 anti-hallucination layers),
  a private-label brochure generator, a film estimator, and a Sales Hub.
- **The company owns what you've built so far.** They paid the subscriptions; you
  built it on their time and data. You already accepted this on purpose — so you
  have clean ground to stand on when you say "from here forward, the terms
  change."
- You have a meeting with the president **this week**. This document is for that
  meeting and the deal that follows it.

**The single most important mindset:** You are not asking for a favor. You are
offering them a deal — early access to something valuable that you can, and will,
take elsewhere. Two things are true at once: you'd *like* this to work with them,
and you *don't need* it to. Both have to be true in your head or the terms will
slip.

## A2. Your leverage — and its expiration date

Your leverage is **not** the code you've already written (they own that). Your
leverage is three things they can't buy off the shelf:

1. **You are the only person who has both the domain knowledge** (you've sold
   flooring, you know the workflows, the reps, the pain) **and the demonstrated
   ability to build agentic systems for it.** Generic software engineers don't
   understand the pain; consultants who understand the pain can't build. You're
   the rare overlap.
2. **You've already de-risked it for them.** They've seen it work in their own
   business. That's worth a fortune in a market where most AI projects are vapor.
3. **Your BATNA is real.** (BATNA = Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement —
   what you do if you walk.) You're being replaced anyway, so you have nothing to
   lose by going independent and building the same thing for *other* distributors.
   **This is the foundation of everything.** Options 1 and 2 below only have value
   to you because Option 3 (walk and build your own) is a genuine, credible
   threat.

**The expiration date:** your leverage is highest *right now*, before you build
anything else for free and before October removes your insider footing. Every
additional free feature you ship without a deal *reduces* your leverage, because
you're giving away the thing you should be selling. **Do not build another line of
code for them without terms in writing.** That instinct you already had is
correct — hold it.

**The trap to avoid:** the moment they sense you *need* this deal because you're
losing your job, your equity and salary numbers collapse. Desperation is the one
thing that can't enter the room. If you feel it, slow down and remember Option 3
is a real, good outcome.

## A3. The routes, ranked honestly for *your* goal (replace income + build wealth)

You framed three. There's a fourth that may be the cleanest. Here they are,
ranked for your actual goal:

### 🥇 Best base case: Option 2 — Salary to build it, WITH a written IP carve-out
They employ you (real salary + ideally a title and runway) to build out the AI
operating layer for the company. **The entire deal lives or dies on one clause:**

> By default, "they pay you to build it" = **work-for-hire = they own 100% of it
> and you have nothing to sell to anyone else.** That is the trap. You must
> negotiate an explicit, written **IP carve-out / license-back**:
> - **They get:** a perpetual license to use everything you build, plus
>   **exclusivity within their lane** (their channel / territory / customer
>   segment — define it narrowly).
> - **You keep:** ownership of the underlying platform and the right to sell it
>   to **non-competing customers** (other distributors outside their territory or
>   segment).

- **Why it's the best base case:** it solves your income problem *immediately*
  (bridges the October cliff), and the carve-out preserves the business you
  actually want to build. Lowest risk, fastest to "I'm safe."
- **Watch out for:** non-competes and IP-assignment language buried in a standard
  employment agreement. A normal offer letter will quietly take everything. This
  is exactly where the lawyer earns their fee.

### 🥈 Cleanest if they want skin in the game: Option 4 — License / revenue-share
You **own** the platform. They become your **first paying customer**: they get
exclusive use in their lane for a license fee and/or a revenue share. You sell
everywhere else.
- **Why it's attractive:** income without giving up equity *or* IP. It also
  reframes you from "employee" to "vendor/partner," which is a stronger long-term
  position. A retainer here can replace your salary while keeping you free.
- **Watch out for:** a distributor may not want to pay "software fees" to a guy
  who was just on payroll. You may need to anchor it against what the labor/error
  cost is today (see A7).

### 🥉 Only at the right price: Option 1 — Partner / JV (they own "half")
They co-own the venture and you sell it together.
- **The critical distinction you must not blur:** *half of what?* **Never give
  away half of Clea Solutions itself.** At most, half of a **separate product
  entity** built around the distribution product. Half of your whole company —
  your brand, your other projects, your future — for one customer's backing is a
  catastrophic trade.
- **Equity must be paid for.** "They own half" is only fair if their half comes
  with real consideration: **cash investment + your salary + a committed anchor
  contract + workable governance.** If all they're contributing is "the use case
  and their name," that's not worth half of anything.
- **The hidden risk:** 50/50 ownership is the classic deadlock structure — when
  you disagree, nobody can break the tie, and the company freezes. And a flooring
  distributor is not a technology operator; they may slow you down, block sales to
  anyone they see as a competitor, or simply not understand the business. **A
  minority strategic stake for them (e.g., 10–25%) tied to real investment is
  almost always better than 50/50.**

### The credible walk-away: Option 3 — Build your own
If they won't do a fair version of 2, 4, or 1, you **stop building for them and
start building for yourself** so you're ready when October hits.
- This is your BATNA and the source of all your leverage. It has to be genuinely
  on the table in your own mind.
- **Constraint to be honest about:** they own the current code, so you can't copy
  it. You'd do a **clean-room rebuild** — your general expertise, your portfolio,
  and your domain knowledge are yours; the specific repos they paid for are not.
  In practice this is a setback of weeks, not a fatal blow, because you now know
  how to build it. Talk to a lawyer about where the line is before you start.

**Your stated anchor going in:** lead toward **Option 2 with the carve-out** (or
the **Option 4 license** if they'd rather not employ you), keep **Option 1
available only at a fair, paid-for, minority-stake price**, and hold **Option 3**
as your real walk-away.

## A4. Red lines & must-gets (decide these *before* you walk in)

- **Everything in writing before you build more.** A handshake is not a deal.
- **IP carve-out is non-negotiable** if you take salary (Option 2). No carve-out,
  no further building — full stop.
- **Never half of Clea Solutions.** Product-entity equity only, and only if paid
  for with real consideration.
- **Exclusivity is scoped, not total.** They can have your lane locked up; they
  cannot lock up the rest of the market. Define "their lane" narrowly (e.g.,
  their product categories + their geography).
- **Salary floor:** your number is **⟨your current total comp, e.g. $X⟩**. Don't
  go below it; you're taking on more skill and more risk, so above it is correct.
- **A runway / commitment term** (e.g., 12–18 months) so they can't have you
  build the thing and then cut you in November.

## A5. Anticipated pushback → your response

- **"We already own everything you've built."**
  → "Correct — and I built it that way on purpose, in good faith. That's the
  proof it works. What we're talking about now is everything that comes *next*,
  which doesn't exist yet, and which I'd build either here or somewhere else."

- **"Why should we pay you for something you were doing anyway?"**
  → "I was proving it could be done. Now it's proven. Building the full operating
  layer is a real job with real value — ⟨quantify: hours saved, errors avoided,
  orders processed⟩ — and I can either do it for you or for other distributors.
  I'd rather do it here first."

- **"Why do you get to sell it to others?"**
  → "Because the alternative is you pay full freight to own something custom, and
  most companies don't want to fund a software company. Letting me sell the
  general version elsewhere is what makes your price reasonable — you get
  exclusivity in *our* market, I cover my costs in others. You're not competing
  with a flooring distributor in ⟨other region/segment⟩."

- **"What if you leave / get hit by a bus?"**
  → Reasonable. Offer documentation, a transition clause, and (if Option 2) a
  defined term. This is a fair concern; address it, don't dodge it.

- **"AI is going to do all this for free in a year anyway."**
  → See Part B, Section B1 — this is the education piece. The frontier labs build
  the *engine*; nobody at OpenAI or Anthropic is going to build *your*
  purchase-order-to-ERP workflow for flooring. That's the gap, and it's not
  closing.

## A6. Walk-away plan (if they say no to all fair versions)

1. **Don't burn the bridge.** "I understand. I'm going to keep building in this
   space. If things change, I'd be glad to talk." Calm, not bitter.
2. **Start the clean-room independent build immediately** — you have until ~Oct
   while you still draw a paycheck. Use that runway.
3. **Line up your first non-{company} distributor** from your network *now*, in
   parallel, so you're not starting from zero in October.
4. **Protect yourself:** know exactly what you can and can't reuse (lawyer), and
   keep your own future work on your own accounts/subscriptions from here on.

# PART B — PROPOSAL FOR ⟨COMPANY⟩ LEADERSHIP

*Prepared by Michael Plante · ⟨date⟩*

*A practical look at where AI is actually going, what we've already built inside
this business, and three ways we could work together to capture it.*

## B1. Why agentic AI — and why now

Most of what you've heard about AI is either breathless hype or dismissive
eye-rolling. The truth sits in a more useful place in the middle, and that middle
is where the money is. Here's the honest version.

### What this technology actually is (in plain English)
- A **large language model (LLM)** — the thing behind ChatGPT — is a system that's
  very good at reading, writing, and reasoning over language. On its own, it's a
  smart assistant that *talks*.
- An **AI agent** is the next step: an LLM that's been given **tools and a job**.
  Instead of just answering a question, it can *act* — read a document, look up a
  price in our catalog, check the math, enter an order, and hand off to a person
  when something looks wrong. The difference between a chatbot and an agent is the
  difference between an employee who can *advise* you and one who can *do the
  task*.

### The two things everyone gets wrong
1. **"AI will do everything within a year."** No. The honest research shows AI is
   powerful but *uneven*. Researchers at Harvard and BCG call this the **"jagged
   technological frontier"**: AI is excellent at some tasks and surprisingly bad
   at others, and the two can look identical from the outside. Used on the wrong
   task, it can actually make output *worse*.
2. **"AI is a toy / a bubble."** Also no. In controlled, real-world studies, the
   gains on the *right* tasks are large and measurable (numbers below).

**The skill that creates value is knowing exactly where that jagged line is** —
which tasks to hand the machine and which to keep with people, and how to build
guardrails so the machine can't quietly get it wrong. That is precisely what we've
been building here, and it's why a system built *for our business* succeeds where
a generic tool fails.

### The evidence (independent, peer-reviewed studies — not vendor marketing)
- **Customer-support agents got ~14% more productive on average — and *novice*
  workers 34% more productive** — in a study of 5,179 real agents by economists at
  Stanford and MIT (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*,
  2025). AI raised the floor: it made average workers perform like the best ones.
- **Consultants on tasks *within* AI's frontier were ~25% faster and produced
  ~40% higher-quality work** — a study of 758 Boston Consulting Group consultants
  with Harvard, Wharton and MIT (*"Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier,"*
  published in *Organization Science*, 2026). The same study found that *outside*
  the frontier, unguided AI use *hurt* performance — which is the whole point:
  deployment skill matters.
- **The honest counter-example:** in a 2025 randomized study by METR, experienced
  open-source developers were actually **19% *slower*** using AI tools on code
  they knew deeply — yet they *believed* they'd been sped up by 20%. The lesson
  isn't "AI doesn't work." It's that **AI must be aimed at the right work, with
  measurement, or it quietly costs you.** That is the discipline we bring.
- For scale, McKinsey estimates generative AI could add **$2.6–$4.4 trillion**
  annually to the global economy. Treat that as a direction, not a promise — but
  the direction is not in doubt.

### Why *now*, and why not "just wait for the big labs to do it"
The frontier AI companies — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google — build the **engine** (the
models). They are not going to build *our* purchase-order-to-ERP workflow for
flooring distribution. That last mile — our catalogs, our SKUs, our reps' habits,
our error-prone steps — is exactly the part they'll never touch, and exactly the
part that creates durable advantage for whoever builds it first. **Waiting doesn't
get us their version of our system; it just hands the first-mover advantage in our
market to someone else.** The cost of doing nothing is not zero — it's the
compounding edge a competitor gets by starting before we do.

## B2. This isn't theory — it's already running in our business

We didn't write a research paper. We built working systems, here, on our own
operations:

- **Purchase-Order Intelligent Document Processing (PO IDP):** turns inbound
  purchase-order PDFs into validated sales orders. It has **seven independent
  anti-hallucination layers** — every value traces back to the exact source text,
  **two separate AI models must agree** before anything is accepted, and the math
  has to reconcile to the cent. Marginal cost is about **$0.0001 per document.**
  → *Value to us:* ⟨# POs/month × time saved, error/rework reduction — fill in⟩.
- **Sales Hub:** a field-ready workspace for reps — voice-first assistant,
  multi-step sample orders, CRM pipeline, and private-label price-crossover
  lookup. → *Value:* ⟨rep hours saved / faster response — fill in⟩.
- **Private-Label Brochure Generator:** scrapes factory product pages and produces
  branded PDF brochures with color-crossover lists in **under 60 seconds** (vs.
  ⟨current time⟩ by hand).
- **Film/Material Estimator:** calculates linear footage, rolls, and costs for
  doors, walls, columns, and custom surfaces.

Every one of these was built with the same discipline: **grounded in our real
data, checked by independent passes, auditable.** Not a chatbot — systems that do
the work and show their work.

## B3. The vision: an AI operating layer for the business

These four tools are the first bricks. The bigger opportunity is to connect them
into a single **AI operating layer** that sits on top of how we already
work — quoting, ordering, sourcing, rep enablement, document handling — and
removes the slow, error-prone, manual steps across the board. Over the next 12–24
months that looks like:

- Every inbound document (POs, spec sheets, vendor confirmations) processed and
  validated automatically.
- Reps freed from lookups and paperwork to do what reps do — sell and build
  relationships.
- Institutional knowledge (pricing, crossovers, product expertise) captured so it
  doesn't walk out the door when someone retires.

## B4. The opportunity beyond us

Here's the part that should make this exciting rather than just useful: **the
problems we're solving are not unique to us.** Every building-products and
construction-materials distributor in the country wrestles with the same
manual-order, manual-quote, tribal-knowledge pain. What works here can be sold to
the broader market — and that is an upside the company can **share in**, depending
on how we structure working together. We can be the first reference customer of a
business that serves an entire industry, instead of just an internal cost center.

## B5. Three ways we could work together

I've built the proof on my own time, in good faith, and the company owns what's
been built so far. What comes next — the full operating layer — is a real
undertaking, and I'd like to build it here first. Here are three honest structures;
I'm open to discussing any of them:

1. **Bring me on to build it (employment + a clear IP arrangement).** You fund the
   build with a role; we agree in writing that the company has full, exclusive use
   of everything within our market, while I retain the ability to offer the
   general version to non-competing companies outside our space. You get a
   custom-built system and a dedicated builder; I get to keep growing the broader
   capability.

2. **License / partnership.** I continue to own and develop the platform; the
   company becomes the anchor customer with exclusive use in our market for a fee
   or revenue share. You get the system without having to run a software effort;
   I cover development by serving other markets.

3. **Co-venture.** We stand up a shared venture around the product, with the
   company taking a strategic stake backed by real investment and commitment, and
   we go to market together.

Each of these can work. The wrong move for both of us is to keep going with no
structure at all.

## B6. What I'd need to move forward

- A short conversation about which of the three directions fits, and a **written
  agreement on the go-forward terms — including IP — before I build the next
  phase.** I want to keep moving fast; I just want us both clear and protected
  before we do.
- I'm ready to start the moment we have that clarity.

## Appendix — Business fundamentals (context for the strategy)

*(Background for Michael; supports Part B but not part of the pitch.)*

- **Vertical focus is the moat, not a limitation.** The durable AI businesses are
  *vertical* — owning one industry's workflows end-to-end. Building-products /
  construction-materials distribution is large, unglamorous, underserved, and
  document-heavy: ideal territory for a domain insider, and territory the frontier
  labs will never enter. Resist the urge to go "horizontal" — that's where you get
  run over.
- **What compounds vs. what gets commoditized.** Models will keep getting better
  and cheaper, which means clever prompting and even today's clever harness will
  erode in value. The assets that *compound* and don't get steamrolled:
  **proprietary domain data and evaluation sets, integrations into customers' ERPs
  and systems, and customer trust/relationships.** Invest deliberately in those.
- **Services → SaaS arc.** Start high-touch (build engagements / managed AI ops)
  to fund the business and earn trust; turn the repeatable wins — PO IDP is the
  strongest wedge — into a productized vertical SaaS you own. Let services pull
  product, not replace it.
- **12-month goal: replace/exceed income ⟨$X⟩.** Whichever route the company
  chooses, the plan is the same shape: secure income (salary, retainer, or first
  paying customers), then layer in 1–3 additional distributor customers so you're
  not dependent on a single relationship. Quarterly targets: ⟨fill in⟩.
- **Risks & kill criteria (be honest with yourself):** model commoditization of
  your specific features; over-concentration in one customer; solo
  non-technical-founder execution and maintenance ceiling (plan for a technical
  first hire/co-founder as revenue allows); burnout. If after ⟨X months⟩ you have
  zero paying customers outside the original company and no clear pipeline, that's
  a signal to reassess, not push harder.
